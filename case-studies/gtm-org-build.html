Building a GTM Systems Org

Hired and scaled a 10-person GTM Systems organization — Salesforce Admins, Developers, and Business Systems Managers — governing a $4M+ annual SaaS stack and establishing the delivery standards, change management, and operational SLAs that kept a hypergrowth GTM motion running.

The Problem

Rapid growth had outpaced the systems function. A single-admin Salesforce environment was running a 400-person GTM motion.

At a high-growth developer tools company scaling from Series C through Series D, the GTM Systems function had never kept pace with headcount. The company had grown from 50 to 400+ in three years — but the systems team supporting the revenue motion was still effectively a single Salesforce admin and a few part-time contributors from IT.

The symptoms of under-investment were everywhere. Sales reps waited weeks for basic CRM changes. Marketing couldn't get data models updated without a backlog of competing requests. CS had built their own workarounds in Zendesk because getting Salesforce changes was too slow. And with a $4M+ annual SaaS stack spread across 20+ tools, there was no centralized function accountable for how that spend was managed or evaluated.

Under-investing in GTM Systems at scale is a tax on the entire revenue org. Every hour a rep spends working around CRM issues, every week a campaign is delayed by a data model gap, every deal that slips because CPQ couldn't handle the pricing structure — these costs are invisible on a systems team headcount plan but very visible in the revenue numbers.
  • Single point of failure: one Salesforce admin supporting a 400-person GTM org, with no backups or specialization
  • No delivery framework: all requests worked informally — no intake, no prioritization, no SLAs
  • Change management gaps: production configuration changes were made ad hoc with no testing environments or release processes
  • Stack without governance: 20+ tools with no centralized ownership, duplicate capabilities, and auto-renewing contracts nobody was reviewing
  • No strategic alignment: systems work was reactive to individual requests rather than mapped to GTM priorities

The Approach

Hire for function, not headcount. Build the operating model before scaling the team.

The temptation when inheriting an understaffed systems function is to hire fast and figure it out later. I did the opposite: before making a single hire, I defined the operating model — what the org would own, how work would flow in, how delivery would be measured, and what the escalation paths looked like. Hiring into a defined structure produces very different results than hiring into a vacuum.

I mapped the team to three functional layers: Administrators (day-to-day CRM configuration, user management, basic automations), Developers (complex customizations, integrations, data pipelines), and Business Systems Managers (stakeholder-facing, project management, requirements gathering). Each layer had defined scope, clear career paths, and distinct hiring profiles.

The operating model came first: intake process, SLA tiers, change management workflow, and a quarterly planning cadence tied to GTM leadership priorities. Only then did we scale headcount — so each hire had a clear role to step into rather than a blank-slate "figure out what's needed" onboarding experience.

The Build

Five pillars: org structure, intake, change management, SLAs, and stack governance.

01
Org structure — Three-tier model with clear scope
Defined three functional layers: Salesforce Admins (configuration, users, automations), Salesforce Developers (Apex, integrations, data engineering), and Business Systems Managers (stakeholder management, project delivery, requirements). Each with hiring rubrics, onboarding paths, and defined scope. Grew from 1 to 10 over 18 months, filling critical capability gaps in sequence.
02
Intake and prioritization — Jira-based request management
Replaced informal Slack/email requests with a structured Jira intake process. Every request captured with business impact, requesting team, and urgency. Weekly triage session with GTM leadership to set priorities. Eliminated the "whoever shouts loudest gets served first" dynamic that had dominated before.
03
Change management — Dev → Staging → Production release process
Built a three-environment Salesforce setup with formal change control: all development in sandbox, QA in full sandbox with real data shapes, production deployments on a biweekly release schedule with rollback documentation. Eliminated the ad hoc production changes that had caused several major incidents before the framework existed.
04
SLA tiers — P1/P2/P3 with defined response windows
Three-tier SLA framework: P1 (revenue-blocking issue — 4-hour response, same-day resolution), P2 (business-impacting but not revenue-blocking — 24-hour response, 3-day resolution), P3 (enhancement or non-urgent request — triaged in weekly session). Published to all GTM leadership. Response rate to SLA: >95% within 6 months.
05
Stack governance — Quarterly reviews and intake controls
Established GTM Systems as the approval gate for all new SaaS tool purchases: business case required, overlap assessment required, budget owner sign-off required. Quarterly stack reviews covering utilization, contract renewals, and tool performance. This governance framework directly fed the $300K+ consolidation project that followed.

Key Takeaways

What I learned building a GTM Systems function from the ground up.

🏗️ Build the operating model before scaling the team. Hiring fast into an undefined function creates chaos at scale. Define what the org owns, how work flows in, and how delivery is measured before you write a single job description. Hiring into structure produces dramatically better outcomes than hiring into ambiguity.
📋 SLAs aren't bureaucracy — they're trust infrastructure. GTM teams lose faith in a systems function when they don't know when their requests will be addressed. Publishing SLA tiers and then hitting them consistently is the fastest way to rebuild credibility with Sales and Marketing leadership.
🔐 Change management is the unglamorous unlock. Most Salesforce environments at high-growth companies are fragile because configuration changes go straight to production. A sandbox-first release process feels slow at first but eliminates an entire category of incidents that erode stakeholder trust.
🔭 Governance creates optionality. When GTM Systems owns the intake process for new tools, you build an inventory of the full stack. That inventory is what enabled the $300K+ consolidation — you can't audit what you don't know you own. Governance isn't just about control; it's about visibility.
