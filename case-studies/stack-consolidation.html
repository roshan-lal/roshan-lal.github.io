GTM Stack Consolidation — $300K+ Saved

A systematic audit of 20+ GTM tools — eliminating shelfware, consolidating overlap, and renegotiating contracts to save $300K+ annually, without losing a single critical capability.

The Problem

Four years of rapid growth, no stack governance. The GTM team was paying for tools nobody was using.

At fast-growing B2B SaaS companies, the GTM stack accumulates the same way technical debt does — one tool at a time, each with a compelling use case at the moment of purchase, and no one reviewing the full picture. By the time I joined Postman, the stack had grown to 20+ active GTM tools across Sales, Marketing, and CS, with a combined annual spend that had never been strategically reviewed.

The symptoms were everywhere: three tools with intent data capabilities, two with conversation intelligence, and a handful of tools that had essentially zero adoption outside the team that bought them. Contracts were auto-renewing year over year without anyone evaluating whether the tool was still being used, let alone whether it was still the best option.

The real cost wasn't just the SaaS spend. It was the cognitive load of a fragmented stack — reps toggling between too many tools, data spread across systems that didn't talk to each other, and RevOps spending cycles supporting tools that shouldn't exist. The cleanup paid dividends beyond the budget line.
  • Stack sprawl: 20+ tools, many with overlapping capability, none with clear ownership accountability
  • Shelfware: multiple tools with active contracts but less than 20% seat utilization
  • Auto-renewal risk: large contracts renewing without review, missing negotiation windows
  • No single view of GTM stack spend — budget was split across Sales, Marketing, and CS cost centers
  • No governance framework to prevent the same sprawl from happening again

The Approach

Build a complete picture before making any decisions. Align stakeholders early. Use data as leverage, not opinion.

The biggest mistake in stack consolidation projects is starting with a conclusion and working backward. I started with a commitment: no tool gets cut or renegotiated until the data supports it. That meant building an audit process that was rigorous enough that stakeholders trusted the findings — even when those findings pointed to their tools.

I used a four-category framework to classify every tool: Keep (high value, high usage, no overlap), Consolidate (valuable capability, but duplicated elsewhere in the stack), Renegotiate (keep the tool, but pricing doesn't reflect current usage or market rate), and Eliminate (low usage, replaceable by existing tools, or genuinely unused).

Critically, I ran this process with stakeholders, not at them. Each category review was a working session with Sales, Marketing, and CS leaders — not a memo from RevOps telling people their tools were going away. That buy-in made the execution smooth.

The Build

A five-phase process: audit, align, negotiate, migrate, govern.

01
Phase 1 — Full stack inventory (4 weeks)
Built a master spreadsheet: every tool, annual contract value, renewal date, seat count purchased, seat count actively used (pulled from usage APIs or IT), primary use case, and business owner. For the first time, one document showed the complete picture across all three GTM functions.
02
Phase 2 — Stakeholder alignment (3 weeks)
Ran category-by-category review sessions with Sales, Marketing, and CS leadership. Presented usage data and overlap mapping. Listened for objections — some "low usage" tools had legitimate reasons; others were just forgotten. Reached alignment on Keep / Consolidate / Renegotiate / Eliminate for every tool before any vendor conversations.
03
Phase 3 — Vendor negotiations (6 weeks)
Entered eight renewal conversations armed with specific data: current usage vs. contracted seats, competing vendor pricing benchmarks, and internal alternatives if they didn't move on price. Used upcoming contract renewals as the natural leverage point — never threatening, always data-led. Achieved meaningful discounts across every renegotiation.
04
Phase 4 — Migration and sunsetting (8 weeks)
Planned orderly sunsetting of 6 tools: data exports, workflow migrations, user communications, and training on replacement tools. No "rug pulls" — every sunset had a 30-day transition period with clear runbooks for affected teams.
05
Phase 5 — Governance framework (ongoing)
Established quarterly GTM stack reviews: usage report for every active tool, renewal calendar with 90-day review windows, and a formal intake process for new tool requests (business case required, alternative evaluation required, RevOps approval required). Stack sprawl doesn't come back if you build the guardrails.

Key Takeaways

What makes a stack audit actually work — and stick.

📊 Usage data is your most powerful asset. Opinion is easy to dismiss. Showing a stakeholder that their team is using 15 of 80 purchased seats — with receipts — is not. Pull the data from every source available (vendor admin portals, IT SSO logs, Salesforce activity) before entering any stakeholder conversation.
🤝 Do this with people, not to them. Stack consolidation fails when it's done as a cost-cutting exercise imposed on teams. When stakeholders are part of the analysis — and their concerns are heard and addressed — they become advocates for the outcome rather than resistors.
💼 Timing is negotiating leverage. The best time to renegotiate a contract is 90 days before renewal — when you still have the credible option to walk. I calendared every renewal date in the first week of the audit and worked backward from those deadlines to create negotiating windows.
🔁 The governance model is the real deliverable. A one-time audit that saves $300K is good. A governance framework that prevents $300K of new waste from accumulating over the next three years is better. Don't end the project at the cost reduction — build the system that keeps it clean.
